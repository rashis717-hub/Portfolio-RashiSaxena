<Section n="01" title="Context & Business Problem" headline="Keeping regulated infrastructure compliant is a cycle that never closes">

### The enterprise landscape

Banks, exchanges, insurers and hospitals run on infrastructure that regulators watch. The people who look after it, IT, infrastructure and DevOps teams, manage servers, networks, Kubernetes clusters and cloud resources, on premises and in the cloud. They also have to prove that all of it is configured, patched and upgraded to a benchmark, again and again.

That work is a loop: harden, patch, upgrade, prove, and then repeat. It never finishes, and it competes with everything else IT is asked to do. Roughly 80% of IT budget goes to running the bank, which leaves about 20% for change.

### The core problem

These teams already had automation: scripts, and orchestrated workflows that run provisioning and patching. The automation worked. What was missing was everything around it.

- A request still began as a chat message or an email, and was coordinated on a bridge call at 23:00 during a night window.
- Approval was informal, so nobody could say quickly who had agreed to what.
- Evidence for the audit was assembled by hand after the change.
- The same process was repeated in every environment, and when drift broke a run, people chased it across tools.

Scripts and orchestration can follow predefined steps, but they cannot interpret the environment they run in, and they do not carry accountability. People filled that gap, at night and on calls.

### Business pain and user pain

| What people experience | What it costs the business |
|---|---|
| Requests arrive by chat and email, and need a bridge call to coordinate | Slow turnaround, and engineers pulled into coordination out of hours |
| Evidence is assembled by hand after the change | Audit effort, and the risk of gaps in the record |
| The same process is repeated in every environment | Inconsistency and rework |
| Drift breaks a run and people chase the status across tools | Failed runs and unplanned work |

The brief for the product was to put the request, the approval, the execution and the proof into one governed place, and to let AI agents take on the investigating, with a person keeping the decision.

### How I researched

Infrastructure compliance is a specialist domain, and I did not begin with a round of interviews. The fastest way to build a reliable picture was to combine two sources and then check them against each other.

**AI-assisted research.** I used AI tools to map the landscape: how regulated organisations run the harden, patch, upgrade and prove cycle, how teams request and approve infrastructure change today, and where the manual gaps appear. I treated the output as a starting point to examine, not as an answer.

**Domain input from Aditya Sharma.** Aditya, CitiusCloud's AI Lead, provided much of the specialist input: how the product and its agents should work, and what the people who run infrastructure care about. That input is what turned a general picture into something specific to infraBOT.

I synthesised the two, together with CitiusCloud's product material, into three things: a map of how change moves today, the needs of the four teams, and three insights. Those became the design principles, the information architecture and the flows that follow.

### What the mapping showed

Three things shaped every decision that followed.

1. **A request has to carry its whole context.** The bridge call exists because a chat message doesn't say what, where, who approved, or when. A guided request can say all of it up front.
2. **Approval is where trust lives.** Everyone's confidence, from the requester to the compliance team, depends on knowing that the right person agreed before anything ran.
3. **Evidence should be a by-product of the run, not a separate task.** If every run reports itself, nobody assembles proof by hand.

### How much to trust these insights

| Insight | Where it comes from | Status |
|---|---|---|
| A request has to carry its whole context | AI-assisted research on how change is requested today, with input from Aditya Sharma | Directional, to validate with operators |
| Approval is where trust lives | AI-assisted research and Aditya's input on how agents and people should share decisions | Directional, to validate with operators |
| Evidence should be a by-product of the run | AI-assisted research and CitiusCloud's product material | Directional, to validate with operators |

This is expert and desk research. It gave me a confident direction quickly, but it is not evidence from end users, so I treat the insights as a strong starting point and not as proof. The next step is to put the request flow in front of working infrastructure operators.

### User flows

The two flows that matter most are raising a request, and an agent raising a ticket. The third is the one administrators use to build everything the others depend on.

<Figure
  src="/work/infrabot/wireframes/09-user-flow-request"
  themed
  size="wide"
  alt="Swimlane user flow for raising a request: the requester's five steps, the approver's decision, and infraBOT running the work and producing a report"
  caption="Raising a request: five guided steps, an approver's decision, then a run and a report. Approval is configured per operation: none, one level or several."
  zoom
/>

Raising a request is five steps: category, operation, parameters, target, and review. Each step answers one question, and the next is only available once it is answered, so a request cannot be submitted incomplete. If the operation needs approval, the approver sees exactly what will run, on which endpoints, and the path it took. They can approve, ask for a change, or reject, and a rejection keeps its reason on the record. After approval the work runs, and every host reports back.

<Figure
  src="/work/infrabot/wireframes/10-user-flow-agent"
  themed
  size="wide"
  alt="Swimlane user flow for an agent-raised ticket: the agent watches, finds an issue, investigates and raises a ticket; an approver decides; an approved fix runs, a root cause analysis is recorded and the knowledge base grows"
  caption="An agent-raised ticket: diagnosis and proposed commands arrive on the ticket, a person decides, and only an approved fix runs."
  zoom
/>

In the agent flow the agent does the investigating and the person keeps the decision. The ticket carries the diagnosis, the root cause and the exact commands, and is flagged as AI-generated so nobody reads it as fact. After an approved fix, a root cause analysis is recorded and added to the knowledge base, where later tickets can cite it.

### Role-based views and edge cases

An operations product earns trust in its edge cases. These are the situations I designed for deliberately.

| Scenario | How the design handles it |
|---|---|
| An operation needs more than one approver | Multi-stage workflows, each stage owned by a team, with approver rules and conditions |
| Different people may request, approve and run | Custom roles with a permission matrix, and self-service access requests |
| Business units need separation | Separate tenants, each with its own data, users, roles, sign-in and branding |
| A run fails on some hosts | Results per host with live output, and failed hosts flagged in the report |
| One rule needs to be skipped on one endpoint | Rule exceptions recorded per endpoint and kept in the evidence |
| An agent's diagnosis could be wrong | An AI-generated banner, the exact commands visible, and a hard approval before anything runs |
| An agent should only observe | Read-only agents for health monitoring and leadership briefings that never act |
| A request affects many endpoints | One request, a run across all targets, and a report for each endpoint |

### Reflections and learnings

Automation was never the hard part. The hard part was trust: who asked, who approved, what ran, and what it proved.

The tension that shaped the work was between speed and governance. Requesters wanted to move quickly, and security and compliance teams needed every change to be traceable. The answer was not a compromise between the two. It was to make governance part of the request, so that the approval path, the target and the evidence are all there from the start.

Next, I want to put the request flow in front of more operators and measure how long a request takes from raised to reported.
